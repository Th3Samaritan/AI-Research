00

How to read the maths

Most of the "vagueness" in quantum mechanics texts is unexplained notation, not unexplained physics. Here is the entire vocabulary used throughout the course. Nothing else appears.

SymbolRead it asWhat it actually is
\(\psi(\mathbf r)\)"psi of r" A function that assigns a number to every point in space. Feed it coordinates, it returns an amplitude. Nothing more mysterious than a temperature field \(T(x,y,z)\).
\(|\psi|^2\)"psi squared" Probability density: \(|\psi|^2\,dV\) is the probability of finding the electron inside the little volume \(dV\). Units: 1/volume.
\(\hat H\)"H-hat", the Hamiltonian An operator: a machine that eats a function and returns a different function. \(\hat H = -\tfrac12\nabla^2 + V\) means "take the second derivative, multiply by −½, then add \(V\) times the original". Not a number.
\(\nabla^2\)"del squared", the Laplacian Sum of unmixed second derivatives, \(\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2+\partial_z^2\). It measures curvature. This is the only place kinetic energy enters.
\(\hat H\psi = E\psi\)"H psi equals E psi" An eigenvalue equation: find the special functions that the operator merely rescales rather than reshapes. \(\psi\) = eigenfunction (the orbital), \(E\) = eigenvalue (its energy).
\(\langle f|g\rangle\)"bracket f g" Shorthand for the integral \(\int f^*(\mathbf r)\,g(\mathbf r)\,d^3r\) over all space. It is an inner product — the continuous version of a dot product between vectors. Real functions here, so drop the complex conjugate.
\(\langle f|\hat H|g\rangle\)"matrix element" Apply the operator to \(g\), then take the inner product with \(f\): \(\int f\,(\hat Hg)\,d^3r\). One number. Stack these numbers in a grid and you have the matrix the computer diagonalises.
\(\int d^3r\)"integrate over all space" For a spherically symmetric integrand this collapses to \(\int_0^\infty 4\pi r^2 f(r)\,dr\) — the \(4\pi r^2\) is the surface area of a shell of radius \(r\), i.e. how much space sits at that distance. Every radial integral below uses this one substitution.
\(\delta_{ij}\)"delta i j" 1 if \(i=j\), 0 otherwise. A compact way of saying "these functions are orthonormal".
The one idea behind all of it: functions behave like vectors. They add, they scale, they have inner products, and operators act on them like matrices. "Solving the Schrödinger equation" is then literally the same task as finding the principal axes of a stress tensor — find the directions the operator doesn't rotate. Every method in this course is a different choice of which vectors to use as the basis.
01

Atomic units — why there are no constants in the code

Look at lesson02_hydrogen_atom.py: no \(\hbar\), no electron mass, no \(4\pi\varepsilon_0\). They didn't vanish — they were set to 1 by choosing the units.

The rule: measure mass in electron masses, charge in electron charges, action in \(\hbar\), and let the Coulomb constant be 1. Four choices, four constants gone:

$$\hbar = m_e = e = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 1$$
QuantityAtomic unitIn SIFeel for it
Length1 bohr, \(a_0\)0.5292 Å = 52.92 pm The most probable radius of hydrogen's 1s electron. A C–C bond is ≈ 2.9 bohr.
Energy1 hartree, \(E_h\)27.211 eV = 2625.5 kJ/mol Twice hydrogen's ionisation energy. A strong covalent bond is ≈ 0.2 hartree.
Mass\(m_e\)9.109×10⁻³¹ kg—

So when the script prints -0.49995, that is −0.49995 hartree = −13.605 eV, and hydrogen's measured ionisation energy is 13.606 eV. The full Schrödinger equation \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla^2\psi - \frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\psi = E\psi\) becomes, in these units, exactly what the code implements:

$$-\tfrac12\nabla^2\psi - \frac{Z}{r}\psi = E\psi$$
Watch out: "1 hartree" and "1 rydberg" are both in circulation (1 Ha = 2 Ry). Quantum ESPRESSO reports rydbergs by default; VASP and most chemistry codes report eV. A factor-of-2 disagreement with a paper is almost always this, not physics.
02

Hydrogen: from three dimensions to one

The equation solved in lesson 2 is the radial equation. Here is where it comes from — every term, including the one that looks invented (the centrifugal barrier).

The derivation

Step 1 — exploit the symmetry

The potential \(V=-Z/r\) depends only on distance, not direction. So look for solutions that separate into "how far" times "which way":

$$\psi(r,\theta,\phi) = R(r)\,Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)$$

\(R(r)\) is the radial function — the part we must solve for numerically. \(Y_{lm}\) are the spherical harmonics: the s, p, d shapes from chemistry. They are universal — the same set appears for any spherically symmetric potential, so they are solved once, analytically, and never again. That is why the code has no angles in it.

Step 2 — the Laplacian in spherical coordinates

The only non-obvious input. Acting on \(R\,Y_{lm}\), the angular derivatives collapse to a single number because the spherical harmonics are themselves eigenfunctions of the angular part, with eigenvalue \(-l(l+1)\):

$$\nabla^2\big(RY_{lm}\big) = Y_{lm}\left[\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) - \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}R\right]$$

Here \(l = 0,1,2,\dots\) is the angular-momentum quantum number (s, p, d). It is an integer for the same reason the box levels were integers: going once around in \(\phi\) must return the same value, so only whole numbers of wavelengths fit around the sphere. Same boundary-condition argument, applied to angle instead of length.

Step 3 — the substitution that removes the first derivative

Define \(u(r) \equiv r\,R(r)\). Then a two-line calculation gives \(\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}(r^2R') = \frac{u''}{r}\). Substituting and multiplying through by \(r\):

$$-\tfrac12 u''(r) + \left[\frac{l(l+1)}{2r^2} - \frac{Z}{r}\right]u(r) = E\,u(r),\qquad u(0)=0$$

This is exactly the 1D equation from lesson 1, on the half-line \(r>0\), with a different potential in the bracket. Nothing new had to be learned to solve it — that is the entire point of the substitution.

Step 4 — why \(u\), not \(R\)

Two payoffs. The equation lost its first-derivative term, so the same three-point stencil as lesson 1 works. And the normalisation becomes flat: \(\int_0^\infty |R|^2 r^2dr = \int_0^\infty |u|^2 dr = 1\) — the \(r^2\) from the volume element is already absorbed into \(u\). So \(|u(r)|^2dr\) reads directly as "probability of being at radius \(r\)", which is what the left panel of the figure plots.

Every term in the bracket

TermNameWhere it came from · what it does
\(-\tfrac12 u''\)kinetic energy From \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\) with \(\hbar=m=1\). Curvature costs energy: a wiggly \(u\) has large \(|u''|\), hence high energy. This is what forbids the electron from collapsing onto the nucleus.
\(-Z/r\)Coulomb attraction Charge \(-1\) (electron) times charge \(+Z\) (nucleus) over distance, with the Coulomb constant equal to 1. Negative = binding. Diverges at \(r\to0\), which is why the grid starts at \(r=dr\), not \(r=0\).
\(\dfrac{l(l+1)}{2r^2}\)centrifugal barrier Not invented — it is the angular part of the kinetic energy, dropped out of step 2. An electron circulating with angular momentum has kinetic energy that grows as it approaches the axis, exactly like \(L^2/2mr^2\) in classical orbital mechanics. It is zero for \(l=0\) (s-states can sit on the nucleus) and a growing wall for p, d.
\(u(0)=0\)boundary condition \(R=u/r\) must stay finite at the origin, so \(u\) must vanish there. In the code this is enforced implicitly: the grid simply has no point at \(r=0\), which is the same "wall" trick as the particle in a box.
The physical payoff, in one line: s-electrons (\(l=0\)) feel no barrier and penetrate to the nucleus; p and d electrons are held out by the \(l(l+1)/2r^2\) wall. In a many-electron atom the penetrating s-electron therefore sees more of the raw nuclear charge — that asymmetry is what orders the periodic table, and it is visible as the right-hand panel of the lesson-2 figure.
03

Hydrogen: build the matrix by hand

Before running lesson02_hydrogen_atom.py, construct the first few entries of the 3000×3000 matrix yourself. Then the code's job becomes obvious rather than magic.

From derivative to matrix

Replace the second derivative by its three-point finite difference on a grid of spacing \(\Delta r\):

$$u''(r_i)\;\approx\;\frac{u_{i+1}-2u_i+u_{i-1}}{\Delta r^{2}}$$

Substitute into \(-\tfrac12u''+Vu=Eu\) and collect the coefficient of each \(u\):

$$\underbrace{\left[\frac{1}{\Delta r^{2}}+V(r_i)\right]}_{\text{diagonal }H_{ii}}u_i \;\underbrace{-\;\frac{1}{2\Delta r^{2}}}_{H_{i,i\pm1}}\big(u_{i+1}+u_{i-1}\big) = E\,u_i$$

That is a tridiagonal matrix — non-zero only on the diagonal and its two neighbours — and it is precisely these two lines of the script:

main = 1.0 / dr**2 + V          # the diagonal,  H_ii
off  = -0.5 / dr**2 * ones(N-1) # the two off-diagonals, H_i,i±1

The actual numbers — check these on your calculator

The script uses rmax = 60, N = 3000, so \(\Delta r = 60/3000 = 0.02\) bohr and the grid is \(r_i = 0.02,\,0.04,\,0.06,\dots\) For the s-channel (\(l=0\)) the potential is just \(-1/r\):

1/dr²          = 1/0.0004        = 2500
off-diagonal   = -0.5/0.0004     = -1250      (every one of them)

i=1: r=0.02   V = -1/0.02  = -50.00   →  H₁₁ = 2500 - 50.00   = 2450.00
i=2: r=0.04   V = -1/0.04  = -25.00   →  H₂₂ = 2500 - 25.00   = 2475.00
i=3: r=0.06   V = -1/0.06  = -16.67   →  H₃₃ = 2500 - 16.67   = 2483.33
i=50: r=1.00  V = -1/1.00  = -1.00    →  H₅₀,₅₀ = 2500 - 1.00 = 2499.00

So the matrix is

$$H=\begin{pmatrix} 2450.00 & -1250 & 0 & \cdots\\ -1250 & 2475.00 & -1250 & \\ 0 & -1250 & 2483.33 & \ddots\\ \vdots & & \ddots & \ddots \end{pmatrix}$$
The thing worth staring at: every entry is in the thousands, yet the answer you want is \(-0.5\). The ground-state energy is a tiny difference between huge kinetic and potential contributions — about one part in 5000. That is why grid spacing matters so much, why quantum chemistry obsesses over numerical precision, and why an eigensolver is used rather than anything cruder. You are extracting a whisper from two shouts that nearly cancel.

A version small enough to solve without a computer

Take the same construction with a deliberately crude grid, \(\Delta r=0.5\) bohr, three points \(r=0.5,1.0,1.5\), \(l=0\). Then \(1/\Delta r^2=4\) and the off-diagonals are \(-2\):

H₁₁ = 4 - 1/0.5 = 4 - 2.000 = 2.000
H₂₂ = 4 - 1/1.0 = 4 - 1.000 = 3.000
H₃₃ = 4 - 1/1.5 = 4 - 0.667 = 3.333

      ⎡ 2.000  -2.000   0.000 ⎤
  H = ⎢-2.000   3.000  -2.000 ⎥
      ⎣ 0.000  -2.000   3.333 ⎦

Its lowest eigenvalue is \(-0.0866\) hartree. Right sign, wildly wrong magnitude — and the two reasons are worth separating, because you will meet both again in every real calculation:

  • Truncation. The grid stops at \(r=1.5\) bohr, but hydrogen's 1s density reaches well past 4 bohr. You have not solved hydrogen — you have solved hydrogen in a box of radius 1.5, and squeezing costs energy exactly as in lesson 1. This dominates here.
  • Resolution. \(\Delta r=0.5\) cannot represent the cusp at the origin, which biases the answer the other way (finite differences under-estimate curvature).

Fix them one at a time and watch the number walk to the answer — this is a convergence test, and you have now done one by hand:

Knowing which knob is currently limiting you is the whole skill of production DFT.

04

The variational theorem, proved

Lesson 3's foundation. Four lines, no hand-waving, and the proof is worth owning because everything after it — Hartree–Fock, DFT, basis sets, plane-wave cutoffs — inherits its logic.

Statement

$$E[\psi] \;=\; \frac{\langle\psi|\hat H|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\;\ge\;E_0 \quad\text{for \textit{any} well-behaved }\psi$$

\(E_0\) is the true ground-state energy. The denominator is there so you don't have to normalise your guess first — it divides out whatever overall scale \(\psi\) happens to have. This ratio has a name: the Rayleigh quotient. Structural engineers meet the identical object when estimating the fundamental frequency of a vibrating structure, and for the identical reason.

Step 1

The exact eigenfunctions \(\phi_n\) of \(\hat H\) form a complete orthonormal set: \(\hat H\phi_n = E_n\phi_n\), \(\langle\phi_m|\phi_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\), with \(E_0\le E_1\le E_2\le\cdots\). So any function can be written \(\psi=\sum_n c_n\phi_n\). We never need to know what the \(\phi_n\) are — only that they exist.

Step 2

Apply \(\hat H\) term by term and use orthonormality to kill every cross term:

$$\langle\psi|\hat H|\psi\rangle=\sum_n |c_n|^2E_n, \qquad \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\sum_n |c_n|^2$$
Step 3

So the Rayleigh quotient is \(E[\psi]=\dfrac{\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n}{\sum_n|c_n|^2}\) — a weighted average of the exact energies, with non-negative weights \(|c_n|^2\).

Step 4

No weighted average can fall below the smallest item being averaged. Hence \(E[\psi]\ge E_0\), with equality only when all the weight sits on \(n=0\) — i.e. when the guess is the ground state. ∎

Why this matters more than it looks: it converts an unsolvable search into an optimisation with a built-in compass. You never know how far you are from the truth, but you always know which of two answers is better: the lower one. Every "improvement" in this field — bigger basis, higher cutoff, more Gaussians — is justified by this one inequality.
The limit of the theorem: it bounds the energy, not other properties. A trial function can give a decent energy and a poor dipole moment or density, because errors in \(\psi\) enter the energy quadratically but other observables linearly. "Good energy" ⇏ "good wavefunction".
05

The three Gaussian integrals, derived

These are the formulas lesson03 hard-codes as overlap(), kinetic() and nuclear(). The walkthrough calls them "standard results". They are three lines of calculus each — here they are.

Setup and the master integrals

The basis functions are s-type Gaussians centred on the nucleus:

$$g_i(\mathbf r)=e^{-a_i r^2}$$

\(a_i\) is the exponent (units: bohr⁻²) and it controls width only — large \(a\) = narrow and spiky, small \(a\) = broad and diffuse. Everything below uses one fact: a product of two Gaussians is another Gaussian, \(g_ig_j=e^{-(a_i+a_j)r^2}\). Write \(p \equiv a_i+a_j\) throughout.

Because everything is spherically symmetric, \(\int d^3r \to \int_0^\infty 4\pi r^2dr\), and only three radial integrals are ever needed:

$$\int_0^\infty re^{-pr^2}dr=\frac{1}{2p},\qquad \int_0^\infty r^2e^{-pr^2}dr=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p^3}},\qquad \int_0^\infty r^4e^{-pr^2}dr=\frac{3}{8}\frac{\sqrt\pi}{p^{5/2}}$$

(The first is elementary by substituting \(s=r^2\); the others follow from the standard \(\int_0^\infty e^{-pr^2}dr=\tfrac12\sqrt{\pi/p}\) by differentiating with respect to \(p\) — each differentiation pulls down a factor \(-r^2\).)

1 · Overlap \(S_{ij}=\langle g_i|g_j\rangle\)

$$S_{ij}=\int e^{-pr^2}d^3r=4\pi\!\int_0^\infty\! r^2e^{-pr^2}dr =4\pi\cdot\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p^3}}=\left(\frac{\pi}{p}\right)^{3/2}$$

What it means physically: how much two basis functions look alike. \(S_{ij}=1\) would mean identical (after normalisation), \(S_{ij}=0\) would mean orthogonal. Gaussians centred on the same nucleus always overlap, which is exactly why the eigenvalue problem later comes out "generalised" rather than ordinary.

2 · Nuclear attraction \(V_{ij}=\langle g_i|-Z/r|g_j\rangle\)

$$V_{ij}=-Z\!\int\frac{e^{-pr^2}}{r}d^3r =-Z\cdot4\pi\!\int_0^\infty\! \frac{r^2}{r}e^{-pr^2}dr =-4\pi Z\cdot\frac{1}{2p}=-\frac{2\pi Z}{p}$$

Note how the \(1/r\) singularity was defused: the volume element supplied \(r^2\), so the integrand is \(r\,e^{-pr^2}\) — perfectly finite. The nucleus's infinite pull is tamed by the vanishing amount of space near it. With \(Z=1\) this is the code's nuclear().

3 · Kinetic energy \(T_{ij}=\langle g_i|-\tfrac12\nabla^2|g_j\rangle\)

Integrate by parts once; the boundary term dies because Gaussians vanish at infinity. This turns a second derivative into a product of first derivatives — numerically and algebraically kinder:

$$T_{ij}=-\tfrac12\int g_i\nabla^2g_j\,d^3r=\tfrac12\int\nabla g_i\cdot\nabla g_j\,d^3r$$

For \(g=e^{-ar^2}\) the gradient is radial, \(\nabla g=-2a\,\mathbf r\,e^{-ar^2}\), so \(\nabla g_i\cdot\nabla g_j=4a_ia_j\,r^2e^{-pr^2}\) and

$$T_{ij}=\tfrac12\cdot4a_ia_j\cdot4\pi\!\int_0^\infty\! r^4e^{-pr^2}dr =8\pi a_ia_j\cdot\frac{3}{8}\frac{\sqrt\pi}{p^{5/2}} =\frac{3\,a_ia_j\,\pi^{3/2}}{p^{5/2}}$$

Sanity check you can do in your head: \(T\) grows with the exponents (narrow = curvy = expensive) and falls with \(p^{5/2}\). Both signs match physical intuition.

Why the industry uses Gaussians: all three results are closed algebraic formulas — no numerical integration anywhere. That property survives when the Gaussians sit on different atoms (a product of two off-centre Gaussians is a third Gaussian on the line between them), which is what makes molecular quantum chemistry tractable. Slater functions \(e^{-\zeta r}\) have the right cusp and the right tail, but no such product rule — which is why STO-3G exists: three Gaussians fitted to one Slater.
06

One Gaussian, minimised on paper

Part A of lesson 3 scans 400 values of \(\alpha\) numerically. You can do it exactly, in five lines, and predict the printed answer to four decimals.

Step 1 — assemble the Rayleigh quotient

With a single basis function, \(i=j\), so \(p=2\alpha\) and the three formulas give

$$S=\left(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}\right)^{3/2},\qquad T=\frac{3\alpha^2\pi^{3/2}}{(2\alpha)^{5/2}},\qquad V=-\frac{2\pi}{2\alpha}=-\frac{\pi}{\alpha}$$
Step 2 — divide (the algebra all cancels)
$$\frac{T}{S}=\frac{3\alpha^2\pi^{3/2}}{2^{5/2}\alpha^{5/2}}\cdot\frac{2^{3/2}\alpha^{3/2}}{\pi^{3/2}}=\frac{3\alpha}{2}, \qquad \frac{V}{S}=-\frac{\pi}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{2^{3/2}\alpha^{3/2}}{\pi^{3/2}}=-2\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}}$$

So the trial energy is a function of one variable:

$$E(\alpha)=\frac{3\alpha}{2}-2\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}}$$

Read it before solving it. First term: kinetic, rises linearly — squeezing costs curvature. Second: attraction, rises like \(\sqrt\alpha\) — huddling near the nucleus pays, but with diminishing returns. A linear cost against a square-root benefit must have a minimum. That competition is the atom: it is what stops the electron collapsing onto the nucleus, and it is the same balance the SCF loop resolves in every later lesson.

Step 3 — minimise 
dE/dα = 3/2 - √(2/(πα)) = 0
    →  √(2/(πα)) = 3/2
    →  2/(πα)    = 9/4
    →  α*        = 8/(9π) = 8/28.2743 = 0.282942
Step 4 — evaluate 
kinetic   T = (3/2)(0.282942)               = +0.424413
potential V = -2√(2×0.282942/π) = -2√0.180126 = -0.848826
              ------------------------------------------------
total     E = -0.424413  hartree   ( = -4/3π exactly )
exact                      -0.500000
error                       0.075587   →  15.1 %
Free professional-grade check — the virial theorem. Any exact Coulomb ground state satisfies \(T=-E\) and \(V=2E\). Ours: \(T=0.4244=-E\) ✓ and \(V=-0.8488=2E\) ✓. A variational optimum inherits this. If you modify the script and the virial relation breaks, your minimisation is wrong — a bug detector that costs nothing.

And the lesson of the 15 % error: it is not a width problem, it is a shape problem. The true 1s orbital \(e^{-r}\) has a cusp at the nucleus (its slope jumps) and a slow exponential tail; \(e^{-\alpha r^2}\) is flat at the origin and dies far too fast at range. No value of \(\alpha\) can fix a wrong functional form — which is precisely the argument for using several Gaussians at once.

07

Why the answer is Hc = ESc

The line eigh(H, S) is the whole of lesson 3 Part B. Here is where that equation comes from — it is ordinary calculus on the Rayleigh quotient.

Step 1 — the trial function is now a mixture
$$\psi(\mathbf r)=\sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i\,g_i(\mathbf r)$$

The \(g_i\) are fixed (chosen once, exponents and all); the \(c_i\) are the unknowns. This is the move that turns quantum mechanics into linear algebra: instead of searching over all conceivable functions, search over \(n\) numbers.

Step 2 — the energy in terms of the coefficients

Insert the sum into the Rayleigh quotient and expand both brackets. Every term is one of the matrix elements from section 05:

$$E(\mathbf c)=\frac{\sum_{ij}c_ic_j\langle g_i|\hat H|g_j\rangle}{\sum_{ij}c_ic_j\langle g_i|g_j\rangle} =\frac{\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}H\mathbf c}{\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}S\mathbf c}, \qquad H_{ij}=T_{ij}+V_{ij},\quad S_{ij}=\langle g_i|g_j\rangle$$
Step 3 — set the gradient to zero

Differentiate with respect to one coefficient \(c_k\), using \(\partial(\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}H\mathbf c)/\partial c_k = 2(H\mathbf c)_k\) for symmetric \(H\), and the quotient rule:

$$\frac{\partial E}{\partial c_k}=\frac{2(H\mathbf c)_k\,(\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}S\mathbf c)-(\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}H\mathbf c)\,2(S\mathbf c)_k}{(\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}S\mathbf c)^2}=0$$

Divide through by \(2\,\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}S\mathbf c\) and recognise \(E=\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}H\mathbf c/\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}S\mathbf c\):

$$\boxed{\;H\mathbf c = E\,S\mathbf c\;}$$

So the stationary points of the energy are the generalised eigenvectors, and their energies are the eigenvalues. The lowest one is your variational estimate of the ground state; the higher ones are (rough) estimates of excited states, free of charge.

ObjectSizeMeaning
\(H_{ij}\)\(n\times n\)Hamiltonian matrix. Entry \(ij\) = the energy coupling between basis functions \(i\) and \(j\).
\(S_{ij}\)\(n\times n\)Overlap matrix. Present only because the basis is non-orthogonal.
\(\mathbf c\)\(n\)The answer: how much of each basis function the orbital contains.
\(E\)scalarThe orbital energy — the eigenvalue.
Why "generalised": if the basis were orthonormal, \(S=I\) and this collapses to the ordinary \(H\mathbf c=E\mathbf c\). The \(S\) is the price of using convenient, non-orthogonal functions. Plane waves are orthogonal — which is one practical reason solid-state codes like them, and why you will not see an overlap matrix in lesson 6.

The Hartree potential — it's just Gauss's law

The one new object in lesson 4. The general definition looks like an obstacle; for a spherical density it collapses into two integrals you already know from first-year electrostatics.

From the definition to the code

Step 1 — what it is

Treat the other electron as a smeared-out cloud of charge with density \(\rho(\mathbf r)\). The electrostatic potential that cloud creates at point \(\mathbf r\) is the sum of Coulomb contributions from every piece of it:

$$v_H(\mathbf r)=\int\frac{\rho(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r'$$

This is not quantum mechanics. It is the same integral you would write for a charged dielectric or a space-charge region in a semiconductor. The quantum content is only in where the density came from.

Step 2 — exploit spherical symmetry (the shell theorem)

For a spherically symmetric cloud, split the charge into the part inside radius \(r\) and the part outside:

  • Inside: by Gauss's law a spherical shell of charge acts, from outside it, as if all its charge sat at the centre. So everything within \(r\) contributes \(Q_{\text{in}}(r)/r\).
  • Outside: the potential inside a uniform shell of radius \(r'\) is constant and equal to \(dq/r'\) — it does not depend on where inside you stand. So each outer shell contributes its own charge divided by its radius.

Writing the radial density as \(n(r)\) — defined so that \(n(r)dr\) is the charge in the shell \([r,r+dr]\), i.e. \(n=4\pi r^2\rho\) and \(\int_0^\infty n\,dr=1\) for one electron:

$$v_H(r)=\underbrace{\frac{1}{r}\int_0^{r}n(r')\,dr'}_{\text{charge inside, seen from outside}} \;+\;\underbrace{\int_r^{\infty}\frac{n(r')}{r'}\,dr'}_{\text{shells outside}}$$
Step 3 — the two lines of code 
inner = np.cumsum(n) * dr                    # ∫₀^r n dr'   — running total outward
outer = np.cumsum((n / r)[::-1])[::-1] * dr  # ∫_r^∞ n/r' dr' — running total inward
return inner / r + outer

The reversed cumsum is the only trick: flip the array, accumulate, flip back — that computes "sum of everything from here to the end" in one pass. Both integrals together cost \(O(N)\), not \(O(N^2)\), which is why this is done in radial form rather than as a double integral.

Two limits to check the formula with, in your head: far outside the cloud (\(r\to\infty\)) the first term becomes \(1/r\) and the second dies — the electron looks like a point charge of 1, correct. At the origin the first term vanishes (no charge inside) and the second is finite — no singularity, which is why the Hartree potential never blows up even though \(1/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|\) does.
The self-interaction footnote that matters later: in this lesson \(n\) is normalised to one electron — the other one — so no electron feels its own field. The general Hartree term used in DFT (lesson 5) instead uses the total density, so each electron does partly repel itself. That deliberate error is self-interaction, and cancelling it is what exchange is for. Watch for the difference between the two lessons; it is not sloppiness, it is the pivot of the whole story.

Why the total energy is not the sum of eigenvalues

The line E_tot = 2*eps - E_ee is the most quietly important formula in lesson 4, and the same logic reappears — with two more terms — in lesson 5. Derive it once and both are yours.

Step 1 — what one eigenvalue contains

The orbital \(u\) satisfies \(\left[-\tfrac12\partial_r^2-\frac{Z}{r}+v_H\right]u=\varepsilon u\). Multiply by \(u\) and integrate (that is, take the expectation value):

$$\varepsilon = \underbrace{\langle u|-\tfrac12\partial_r^2|u\rangle}_{t} + \underbrace{\langle u|-Z/r|u\rangle}_{v_{ne}} + \underbrace{\langle u|v_H|u\rangle}_{J}$$

So an eigenvalue is one electron's kinetic energy + its attraction to the nucleus + its repulsion from the other electron.

Step 2 — add the two electrons up naively
$$2\varepsilon = 2t + 2v_{ne} + 2J$$

The kinetic and nuclear terms are correctly doubled: there really are two electrons, each with its own kinetic energy and its own attraction to the nucleus.

Step 3 — spot the error

But \(J\) is the interaction energy of the pair. Electron 1 repelling electron 2 and electron 2 repelling electron 1 are not two separate energies — they are one interaction, seen from both ends. The true energy contains \(J\) once:

$$E_{\text{total}} = 2t+2v_{ne}+J$$
Step 4 — subtract the surplus
$$E_{\text{total}} = 2\varepsilon - J,\qquad J=\int n(r)\,v_H(r)\,dr$$

which is exactly E_tot = 2*eps - E_ee. The correction is not a fudge — it is the removal of a specific, countable duplication.

Check it with the converged numbers

            E_ee   =  1.02862     (the pair repulsion, counted once)

and the naive alternative, for contrast:
2ε alone    = -1.82790            ← wrong by more than 1 hartree (28 eV)
Carry this warning into every code you ever use. Sums of Kohn–Sham eigenvalues ("band energy") are not total energies, and the difference is enormous. When a DFT output lists both, they differ for precisely this reason. Anyone who compares the eigenvalue sums of two structures and calls the difference a formation energy has made this mistake.

The SCF loop as a fixed-point iteration

"Mix the density" is presented everywhere as a practical trick. It is actually a well-defined numerical method with a convergence criterion you can predict — and the helium run obeys it to two decimal places.

Step 1 — name the map

One SCF cycle takes a density in and gives a density out: build \(v_H[n]\), solve for the orbital, square it. Call that whole operation \(F\). Self-consistency means finding a fixed point:

$$n^{*}=F[n^{*}]$$

This is the same class of problem as solving \(x=\cos x\) by typing cos repeatedly into a calculator, or a Newton–Raphson stress-strain loop in an FE solver.

Step 2 — why plain iteration can fail

Feeding the output straight back in, \(n_{k+1}=F[n_k]\), converges only if \(F\) is a contraction. Near the fixed point, write the error \(e_k=n_k-n^*\) and linearise: \(e_{k+1}\approx \lambda e_k\), where \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of the response (how strongly the output density reacts to a wiggle in the input). If any \(|\lambda|>1\) — and in metals it can be large and negative, meaning charge sloshes back and forth — the loop diverges.

Step 3 — mixing, and what β actually does
$$n_{k+1}=(1-\beta)\,n_k+\beta\,F[n_k]\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad e_{k+1}=\big[(1-\beta)+\beta\lambda\big]\,e_k$$

So the per-iteration error factor is \(|1-\beta+\beta\lambda|\), and it must be below 1. Read off the three regimes:

SituationBehaviourWhat to do
\(\lambda\approx0\) (weak response)\(\beta=1\) converges almost immediatelyTake big steps; small molecules behave like this
\(\lambda\) large and negative (charge sloshing)\(\beta=1\) overshoots and oscillatesSmall \(\beta\): slow but stable
\(\beta\) pushed too high\(|1-\beta+\beta\lambda|>1\): divergenceThis is the widget on the course page, and a real failure mode

Correlation energy — and how much of the error is really physics

The lesson's headline is "the missing energy is correlation". That is true, but the script's number contains something else as well, and separating the two is a skill worth more than the fact.

What correlation means, precisely

Mean field says: electron 1 moves in the average cloud of electron 2. Reality says: when electron 1 is on the left, electron 2 is preferentially on the right — they dodge each other in real time. That instantaneous avoidance lowers the repulsion below the mean-field estimate. The energy you fail to capture by averaging is, by definition,

$$E_c \;\equiv\; E_{\text{exact}} - E_{\text{Hartree–Fock}}$$

It is a definitional quantity, not a measured one: "whatever the best possible mean-field theory still misses".

Why this lesson is secretly Hartree–Fock. Helium's two electrons occupy the same spatial orbital with opposite spins. For that closed-shell case, the exchange term in Hartree–Fock exactly cancels the self-interaction in the Coulomb term, leaving each electron feeling precisely one other electron's density — which is what this code implements. So the converged answer is the Hartree–Fock limit, and the gap to experiment is pure correlation. That coincidence does not survive to bigger atoms.

Lesson 5's model: why the Coulomb law is softened

Before the DFT machinery, understand the toy — because two of its constants look arbitrary and are not.

The problem with 1D Coulomb

In three dimensions the \(1/r\) singularity is harmless: the volume element \(4\pi r^2\) vanishes fast enough to make every integral finite (you saw exactly that in section 05). In one dimension there is no such rescue — \(\int dx/|x|\) diverges logarithmically, and the ground state of a strict 1D Coulomb well has no finite energy.

The standard repair
$$\frac{1}{|x|}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}$$

Finite at the origin (equal to 1), and indistinguishable from \(1/|x|\) once \(|x|\gg1\). The "1" is a softening length in bohr — it stands in for the transverse extent that a real 3D orbital would have. This is the standard device in the 1D-model literature, not a shortcut invented for this course.

Where it appears three times, consistently 
v_ext = -1/√((x-d/2)²+1) - 1/√((x+d/2)²+1)   # electron ← nuclei
K     =  1/√((xᵢ-xⱼ)²+1)                     # electron ↔ electron
E_nn  =  1/√(d²+1)                           # nucleus ↔ nucleus

All three interactions must use the same softening. If the nuclear repulsion used bare \(1/d\) while the electrons felt a softened attraction, the binding curve would be wrong — the terms would no longer cancel correctly at large \(d\). Consistency of the model matters more than the realism of any one piece.

Reading the Hartree line

v_H = K @ n * dx is the discretised integral \(v_H(x)=\int K(x,x')\,n(x')\,dx'\): row \(i\) of the matrix dotted into the density, times the grid spacing. A matrix–vector product is a numerical integral whenever the matrix holds a kernel.

Honest limits of the toy. Energies from this model are not comparable to real H₂ (whose bond length is 1.40 bohr and binding energy 0.17 hartree). What transfers is the machinery and the shape of the result: a minimum exists, charge piles up between the nuclei, and switching off exchange changes both. Never quote a number from a 1D soft-Coulomb model as if it described a molecule.

Exchange: the functional, and what "functional derivative" means

Two lines of lesson 5 carry the entire idea of density functional theory. They are also the two lines every course states without justification.

What a functional is, and what its derivative is

A function eats a number and returns a number. A functional eats an entire function and returns a number — for instance "the total exchange energy of this density". Square brackets signal it: \(E_x[n]\).

Its functional derivative \(\delta E_x/\delta n(x)\) answers: if I add a tiny bump of density at position \(x\), how much does the total energy change, per unit of density added? Discretise the density into grid values \(n_i\) and it is nothing but the partial derivative \(\partial E/\partial n_i\), divided by the grid spacing so it stays finite as the grid refines. That is the whole concept — an ordinary gradient, with one component per point in space instead of one per variable.

Why it matters: a potential is the functional derivative of an energy. That is the rule that turns any proposed energy expression \(E_{xc}[n]\) into the potential \(v_{xc}\) you can actually put into the Kohn–Sham equation. Every functional on Jacob's ladder is delivered as an energy; every code needs the derivative.

Deriving the code's v_x from its E_x

The local density approximation for exchange is

$$E_x[n]=-\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{3}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}\!\int n(x)^{4/3}\,dx \;=\;C\!\int n^{4/3}dx$$

Because the integrand depends on \(n\) at that point only — that is what "local" means — the functional derivative is just the ordinary derivative of the integrand:

δE_x/δn  =  C · d(n^4/3)/dn  =  C · (4/3) · n^1/3

with     C = -(3/4)·(3/π)^1/3 :

  v_x = -(3/4)·(4/3)·(3/π)^1/3 · n^1/3
      =        -(3/π)^1/3 · n^1/3
      =        -(3n/π)^1/3

which is precisely v_x = -np.cbrt(3.0 * n / np.pi). The two lines in the script are an energy and its own derivative — they are not independent choices, and if you modify one you must modify the other.

A free consistency check. Since \(v_x\propto n^{1/3}\) and \(E_x\propto\int n^{4/3}\), the two are rigidly related: \(\int n\,v_x\,dx = \tfrac43 E_x\), i.e. \(E_x=\tfrac34\int n v_x\,dx\). In the converged \(d=2\) run: \(\int n v_x=-1.49084\) and \(0.75\times(-1.49084)=-1.11813=E_x\) — exact to all printed digits. If you ever swap in a different exchange functional and this identity still holds, you forgot to update one of the two lines.
Stated honestly: this \(E_x\) is the three-dimensional uniform-electron-gas result, used in a one-dimensional toy. It plays the structural role of an xc functional so you can watch the machinery work; its numbers are illustrative. The real 1D analogue has a different exponent — and the fact that you can swap the functional without touching anything else in the code is the modularity that makes Jacob's ladder possible.

The total-energy assembly, line by line

This is the formula flagged in the course as the most hand-waved thing in the lesson:
E_tot = N_ELEC*eps[0] - E_H - V_x_int + E_x + E_nn. Every term is forced; here is why.

Step 1 — what the eigenvalue sum contains

The Kohn–Sham orbital satisfies \(\left[-\tfrac12\partial_x^2+v_{\text{eff}}\right]\varphi=\varepsilon\varphi\) with \(v_{\text{eff}}=v_{\text{ext}}+v_H+v_x\). Take the expectation value and multiply by the occupation \(N=2\), using \(n=2|\varphi|^2\):

$$2\varepsilon = T_s + \int n\,v_{\text{ext}}\,dx + \int n\,v_H\,dx + \int n\,v_x\,dx$$

where \(T_s\) is the kinetic energy of the fictitious non-interacting electrons.

Step 2 — what the true energy contains
$$E = T_s + \int n\,v_{\text{ext}}\,dx + \underbrace{\tfrac12\!\int n\,v_H\,dx}_{E_H} + E_x + E_{nn}$$

Note the two mismatches against step 1. The Hartree energy carries a factor \(\tfrac12\) (the same pair-counting argument as section 11 — the loop over pairs counts each pair twice). And the exchange energy \(E_x\) is not \(\int n v_x\): the potential is a derivative of the energy, so they differ by construction.

Step 3 — subtract what was over-counted, add what is missing 
E = 2ε − ∫n v_H − ∫n v_x        (remove both potential terms from the eigenvalue sum)
      + E_H + E_x               (put back the correct energies)
      + E_nn                    (the nuclei repel each other; no electron knows this)

and since ∫n v_H = 2E_H, the first correction collapses:

E = 2ε − E_H − ∫n v_x + E_x + E_nn

Line for line, that is the code. The pieces are usually called "double-counting corrections", which undersells them: they are the difference between an energy and the sum of the eigenvalues of the operator built from it.

Bloch's theorem and the plane-wave matrix element

Lesson 6 writes down a Hamiltonian matrix in three lines of code. Here is where each entry comes from — and why this basis has no overlap matrix.

Step 1 — what periodicity forces

The crystal potential repeats: \(V(x+a)=V(x)\). Translating by one lattice vector therefore cannot change any measurable quantity, so it can only multiply the wavefunction by a phase. Requiring consistency over many translations forces the phase to be \(e^{ika}\), and Bloch's theorem follows:

$$\psi_k(x)=e^{ikx}u_k(x),\quad u_k(x+a)=u_k(x) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \psi_k(x)=\sum_G c_G\,e^{i(k+G)x}$$

The two forms are the same statement: expanding the periodic part \(u_k\) in its Fourier series produces the sum on the right, where \(G=2\pi m/a\) are the reciprocal lattice vectors — precisely the wavevectors whose waves share the lattice's period.

Consequence that makes solids computable: an infinite crystal reduces to one unit cell plus a label \(k\), and \(k\) only needs to run over one Brillouin zone \([-\pi/a,\pi/a]\) — because \(k\) and \(k+G\) give the same set of plane waves.

Step 2 — the kinetic energy is diagonal

Plane waves are eigenfunctions of the kinetic operator, which is the entire reason to use them:

$$-\tfrac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}e^{i(k+G)x}=\tfrac12(k+G)^2\,e^{i(k+G)x} \;\Longrightarrow\; T_{GG'}=\tfrac12(k+G)^2\,\delta_{GG'}$$

Code: H = np.diag(0.5 * (k + G)**2).

Step 3 — the potential is its Fourier series

A matrix element between two plane waves is

$$\langle k+G|V|k+G'\rangle=\frac{1}{a}\int_0^a e^{-i(k+G)x}V(x)e^{i(k+G')x}dx =\frac{1}{a}\int_0^a V(x)\,e^{-i(G-G')x}dx = V_{G-G'}$$

— the Fourier coefficient of the potential at the difference vector. Nothing else survives, because plane waves are orthogonal. For the lesson's potential,

$$V(x)=V_0\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)=\frac{V_0}{2}\left(e^{i2\pi x/a}+e^{-i2\pi x/a}\right)$$

so the only non-zero coefficients are \(V_{\pm2\pi/a}=V_0/2\). Each plane wave couples to its two immediate neighbours in \(G\) and to nothing else — which is exactly the loop

for m in range(nG - 1):
    H[m, m+1] += V0 / 2
    H[m+1, m] += V0 / 2
Why there is no overlap matrix here. In lesson 3 the Gaussians were non-orthogonal, so you had to solve \(H\mathbf c=E S\mathbf c\). Plane waves are orthonormal, \(\langle k+G|k+G'\rangle=\delta_{GG'}\), so \(S=I\) and the problem is the ordinary \(H\mathbf c=E\mathbf c\) — np.linalg.eigvalsh(H), no second argument. That simplification is one of the main reasons solid-state codes are built on plane waves.

Where the gap comes from, and what the cutoff is

Two results you can predict on paper and then check against the printout — including one that deliberately disagrees, for a reason worth knowing.

The gap at the zone boundary — degenerate perturbation theory

Step 1 — find the degeneracy

At the zone edge \(k=-\pi/a\), take the plane waves \(m=0\) (so \(G=0\)) and \(m=+1\) (so \(G=2\pi/a\)). Their free-electron energies with \(a=1\):

m = 0:  ½(k+G)² = ½(-π + 0)²  = ½π² = 4.9348
m = 1:  ½(k+G)² = ½(-π + 2π)² = ½π² = 4.9348      ← exactly degenerate

Two states with the same energy, and \(V\) couples precisely this pair — because they differ by \(G=2\pi/a\), the one non-zero Fourier component.

Step 2 — diagonalise the 2×2
$$\begin{pmatrix}E_0 & V_0/2\\ V_0/2 & E_0\end{pmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; E_\pm=E_0\pm\frac{V_0}{2},\qquad \text{gap}=2\cdot\frac{V_0}{2}=V_0$$

The lower state is the standing wave that piles charge on the ions (it sees more attraction); the upper one piles charge between them. The energy difference between those two ways of standing still is the band gap.

At \(V_0=4\) the two-state prediction is good to 0.25 %; at \(V_0=12\) it is off by 2.2 %, and both levels have been dragged downward. The reason is that the 2×2 treatment ignores coupling to the other plane waves, which the full \(15\times15\) matrix includes: every additional coupling pushes the level down (second-order perturbation theory always lowers the ground state). The approximation degrades as \(V_0\) grows relative to the spacing between free-electron levels — which is the honest statement of when "nearly-free-electron" reasoning applies to a real material.

What the plane-wave cutoff actually is

The script keeps \(m=-M\ldots M\) with \(M=7\): fifteen plane waves. A real code does not ask for a count — it asks for an energy cutoff, and keeps every plane wave below it:

$$\tfrac12|\mathbf k+\mathbf G|^2 < E_{\text{cut}}$$
largest G kept:  2π·7/a = 43.98 bohr⁻¹   (a = 1)
equivalent cutoff: ½(43.98)² = 967 hartree ≈ 26.3 keV

Absurdly high — because \(a=1\) bohr is a toy cell far smaller than any real lattice constant (silicon: 10.26 bohr). The point is the correspondence: "number of plane waves" and "energy cutoff" are the same knob, and in three dimensions the count grows as \(N_{PW}\propto V_{\text{cell}}\,E_{\text{cut}}^{3/2}\). Reduce \(M\) in the script and the low bands go wrong first at large \(|k|\) — you have just run the convergence test that ecutwfc exists for.
