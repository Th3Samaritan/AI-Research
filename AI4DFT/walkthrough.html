Lesson 1

The Schrödinger Equation as an Eigenvalue Problem

lesson01_schrodinger_1d.py

Goal: see with your own eyes that 'solving quantum mechanics' means 'diagonalizing a matrix', and meet the three model potentials the whole course builds on. Everything in DFT — up to and including VASP runs on clusters — is this lesson scaled up.

The equation, symbol by symbol

$$-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\psi''(x) \;+\; V(x)\,\psi(x) \;=\; E\,\psi(x)$$
SymbolMeaning
psi(x)the wavefunction. |psi(x)|2 dx = probability of finding the electron between x and x+dx. It is the unknown we solve for.
psi''(x)its second derivative — curvature. The kinetic-energy term: the more a wavefunction wiggles, the more kinetic energy it carries.
V(x)the potential-energy landscape the electron lives in (chosen by us in each part).
Ethe energy eigenvalue. The equation has normalizable solutions only for special discrete values of E — that is where quantization comes from; nobody puts it in by hand.
the 1/2in SI units this term is hbar2/2m. We work in atomic units: hbar = 1, electron mass = 1, so the prefactor is 1/2. Energies then come out in hartree (1 hartree = 27.211 eV) and lengths in bohr (0.529 angstrom). Chosen purely to remove constant-juggling.

How it becomes a matrix

Put psi on a grid of points spaced dx apart and replace the derivative with the 3-point finite-difference stencil (identical to the PDE track's heat-equation stencil):

$$\psi''(x_i) \approx \frac{\psi_{i+1} - 2\psi_i + \psi_{i-1}}{dx^2}$$

Substituting turns the equation into H psi = E psi with a tridiagonal matrix H. Multiply the stencil by the -1/2 out front and you get exactly what the code builds: diagonal entries 1/dx2 + V(xi) (the -2 in the stencil times -1/2 gives +1/dx2), off-diagonal entries -1/(2 dx2). The grid endpoints are treated as infinite walls (psi = 0 there) simply by not having rows beyond them. scipy.linalg.eigh_tridiagonal then returns eigenvalues (energies) and eigenvectors (orbitals). Each eigenvector is divided by sqrt(dx) so that sum(psi2) dx = 1 — total probability one.

Lesson 2

The Hydrogen Atom

lesson02_hydrogen_atom.py

Goal: solve the one real atom with exact answers, understand the quantum numbers n and l, and see the 'accident' whose breakdown creates the periodic table.

From 3D to 1D: the radial equation

The hydrogen potential V = -Z/r is spherically symmetric, so the 3D wavefunction factorizes as psi(r, theta, phi) = R(r) Ylm(theta, phi): a radial part times a spherical harmonic (the s/p/d angular shapes from chemistry class — those are universal, not hydrogen-specific). Substituting u(r) = r R(r) turns the 3D problem into a 1D one on the half-line r > 0:

$$-\tfrac{1}{2}u''(r) + \left[\frac{l(l+1)}{2r^2} - \frac{Z}{r}\right]u(r) = E\,u(r),\qquad u(0)=0$$
SymbolMeaning
u(r) = r R(r)the reduced radial function. Handy because u(r)2 dr is directly the probability of finding the electron at radius r (the r2 from the spherical volume element is already absorbed).
Znuclear charge: number of protons. 1 for hydrogen; set it to 2 for He+ in the tasks.
-Z/rCoulomb attraction between electron (charge -1) and nucleus (charge +Z) — in atomic units the Coulomb constant is exactly 1.
langular-momentum quantum number: l = 0, 1, 2 = s, p, d. Comes from the angular factorization — each l is an independent 1D problem.
l(l+1)/(2r^2)the centrifugal barrier: the angular part of the kinetic energy. An electron with angular momentum cannot sit at r = 0, so this term pushes high-l orbitals outward — the physical root of s-electron 'penetration'.
u(0) = 0boundary condition: R = u/r must stay finite at the origin.

The exact spectrum is En = -Z2/(2n2) hartree for n > l — the same result Bohr guessed in 1913 and the ODE proves properly. Two things to notice: energies do not depend on l (the 'accidental degeneracy', special to a pure -1/r potential), and they scale as Z2 (core electrons of heavy elements are ferociously bound — the reason Lesson/Chapter 4 freezes them into pseudopotentials).

Lesson 3

The Variational Principle and Basis Sets

lesson03_variational_basis_sets.py

Goal: the theorem that makes guessing rigorous, and the 'basis set' idea that turns quantum mechanics into linear algebra of small matrices. This is the load-bearing wall under Hartree-Fock, DFT, and all of quantum chemistry.

The theorem

$$E[\psi_{\rm trial}] \;=\; \frac{\langle\psi_{\rm trial}|H|\psi_{\rm trial}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{\rm trial}|\psi_{\rm trial}\rangle} \;\ge\; E_0$$

Why it is true (worth internalizing): expand any trial function in the exact eigenstates, psi = sum cn phin. Then E[psi] = sum |cn|2 En / sum |cn|2 — a weighted average of the exact energies. No average can be below the smallest term. Equality holds only if the guess IS the ground state. Consequence: lower energy = strictly better wavefunction, so 'guess and minimize' is not a hack, it is a rigorous algorithm with a built-in compass.

Part A — one Gaussian, one knob

Trial function psi = exp(-alpha r2). The parameter alpha (units bohr-2) controls width: large alpha = narrow and spiky, small alpha = wide and flat. Plugging into E[psi] and doing the (standard) Gaussian integrals gives a closed form:

$$E(\alpha) = \frac{3\alpha}{2} \;-\; 2\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}}$$

Read the two terms physically: the first is kinetic energy — it grows with alpha because narrower means curvier. The second is the Coulomb attraction won by huddling near the nucleus. The competition has an optimum: set dE/d alpha = 0 and you get alpha* = 8/(9 pi) = 0.2829 and E* = -4/(3 pi) = -0.4244 hartree. The exact answer is -0.5, so the BEST possible single Gaussian is off by 15%. The reason is shape: the true 1s orbital e-Zr has a sharp cusp at the nucleus and a slow exponential tail; a Gaussian is flat at the origin and dies too fast at range. No amount of width-tuning fixes the wrong shape.

Part B — basis sets: fix the shape with a team

Write psi = sum ci gi with several Gaussians of different widths. Minimizing E over the coefficients ci (linear algebra, no calculus of variations needed) leads to the generalized eigenvalue problem

$$H\,\mathbf{c} = E\,S\,\mathbf{c}$$

where S is the overlap matrix Sij = integral of gi gj — it appears because Gaussians are not orthogonal to each other, and it is exactly what scipy.linalg.eigh(H, S) solves. The matrix elements have closed forms (this is WHY the industry adopted Gaussians: every needed integral is analytic — Szabo & Ostrund's appendix lists them):

$$S_{ij}=\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{a_i+a_j}\Big)^{3/2},\quad T_{ij}=\frac{3\,a_i a_j\,\pi^{3/2}}{(a_i+a_j)^{5/2}},\quad V_{ij}=\frac{-2\pi Z}{a_i+a_j}$$

Lesson 4

Helium and the Self-Consistent Field Loop

lesson04_hartree_helium_scf.py

Goal: meet electron-electron repulsion, solve it with the mean-field trick, and run the SCF loop — the algorithm that beats at the heart of every DFT code. Also: measure the correlation energy, the error DFT was invented to capture cheaply.

The equation each electron obeys

$$-\tfrac{1}{2}u''(r) + \Big[-\frac{Z}{r} + V_H(r)\Big]u(r) = \varepsilon\,u(r)$$
SymbolMeaning
Z = 2helium's nuclear charge (two protons).
V_H(r)the Hartree potential: the classical electrostatic repulsion from the OTHER electron's charge cloud. This is the new term relative to hydrogen.
epsthe orbital energy of one electron moving in nucleus + partner-cloud.
u(r)the shared 1s radial orbital — both electrons occupy it with opposite spins, which the Pauli principle allows.

For a spherical cloud, VH has a beautiful closed form via the shell theorem you know from electrostatics: charge INSIDE radius r acts like a point charge at the center, and every shell OUTSIDE r contributes a constant potential equal to its charge over its radius:

$$V_H(r) \;=\; \frac{1}{r}\int_0^r n(r')\,dr' \;+\; \int_r^\infty \frac{n(r')}{r'}\,dr'$$

with n = u2 the partner's density (one electron's worth, normalized to 1). In the code both integrals are running sums: np.cumsum(n)*dr for the inner charge and a reversed cumsum for the outer shells. Cheap, exact to grid accuracy.

The chicken-and-egg, and the loop that solves it

VH depends on the orbital, which depends on VH. The resolution is iteration: guess the density → build VH → solve for the orbital → make the new density → mix → repeat until nothing changes. The 'mix' step is load-bearing: taking the new density whole can overshoot and oscillate, so we blend n ← (1-beta) n + beta nnew. When the density stops changing, the field the electrons feel is the field they generate: self-consistent.

Total energy: the double-counting subtlety

$$E_{\rm total} = 2\varepsilon - E_{ee},\qquad E_{ee}=\int n(r)\,V_H(r)\,dr$$

Why subtract? Each orbital energy eps already contains that electron's repulsion with its partner. Adding two eps's therefore counts the one pair interaction TWICE; subtracting Eee once fixes it. Every electronic-structure code carries corrections of exactly this shape (you will see them again, itemized, in Lesson 5).

Lesson 5

Build a Toy Kohn-Sham DFT Code

lesson05_kohn_sham_1d.py

Goal: assemble every moving part of an actual DFT calculation — external, Hartree and exchange-correlation potentials, the SCF loop, the total-energy bookkeeping — in ~100 lines of numpy, and watch it predict a chemical bond. This is the centerpiece of the course: after this, production DFT is 'the same machine, bigger'.

The Kohn-Sham equations

$$\Big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + v_{\rm ext}(x) + v_H[n](x) + v_{x}[n](x)\Big]\varphi_i = \varepsilon_i \varphi_i, \qquad n(x) = \sum_i^{\rm occ} 2\,|\varphi_i(x)|^2$$
SymbolMeaning
phi_ithe Kohn-Sham orbitals — orbitals of the FICTITIOUS non-interacting electrons. Individually they are mathematical scaffolding; their summed density is the physical object.

The two model choices, stated honestly

Soft Coulomb. In 1D the true 1/|x| interaction is pathological (its integrals diverge), so the standard 1D-atom literature softens it to 1/sqrt(x2 + 1). The '+1' removes the singularity and sets a length scale of about one bohr. Both electron-nucleus attraction, electron-electron repulsion, AND the nucleus-nucleus term use the same softened kernel — consistency matters more than the particular smoothing.

The exchange functional. We use the Dirac/Slater local-density exchange, derived in 1930 for the 3D uniform electron gas:

$$E_x[n] = -\tfrac{3}{4}\Big(\tfrac{3}{\pi}\Big)^{1/3}\!\!\int n^{4/3}dx\;\;\Rightarrow\;\; v_x(x) = \frac{\delta E_x}{\delta n} = -\Big(\tfrac{3\,n(x)}{\pi}\Big)^{1/3}$$

'Local density approximation' means exactly this: at each point, pretend the material is a uniform gas of that local density and use the gas's known exchange energy. Honest caveat, also printed in the code: that formula belongs to 3D; in our 1D toy it plays the ROLE of an xc functional so you experience the machinery. The numbers are illustrative, not publishable — which is itself a real DFT lesson: the functional is a modeling choice you must declare.

Total energy: every correction itemized

$$E = \sum_i^{\rm occ}\varepsilon_i \;-\; E_H \;-\; \int n\,v_x\,dx \;+\; E_x \;+\; E_{nn}$$

Why each term: the eigenvalue sum contains the full Hartree interaction TWICE (each orbital feels the whole cloud, Lesson 4's double counting), so subtract EH = (1/2) integral n vH once. The eigenvalues also contain integral n vx, but the true functional contributes Ex — so remove the one and add the other. Finally Enn = 1/sqrt(d2+1) is the nuclei repelling each other (softened, consistently). This exact bookkeeping, term for term, appears in every DFT code's total-energy output block — open a Quantum ESPRESSO log later and you will recognize the lines.

Crystals: Band Structures from Plane Waves

lesson06_bands_planewaves.py

Goal: go from one atom to 1023: Bloch's theorem, the plane-wave basis every big materials code uses, band gaps opening, and the counting rule that separates metals from semiconductors.

Bloch's theorem and the basis

In a periodic potential V(x + a) = V(x), eigenstates are labeled by a wavevector k confined to the Brillouin zone k in [-pi/a, +pi/a], and each state is a sum of plane waves whose wavevectors differ from k by reciprocal-lattice vectors G = 2 pi m / a (integer m) — only those keep the required periodicity:

$$\psi_k(x) = \sum_G c_G\, e^{i(k+G)x}, \qquad G = \frac{2\pi m}{a}$$

In this basis the Hamiltonian is a small matrix per k. Kinetic energy is diagonal: (1/2)(k+G)2. The potential enters through its Fourier components; for V = V0 cos(2 pi x/a) the only components are V0/2 at G' - G = +/- 2 pi/a, so the matrix couples each plane wave to its immediate neighbors:

$$H_{GG'}(k) = \tfrac{1}{2}(k+G)^2\,\delta_{GG'} + \tfrac{V_0}{2}\,\delta_{|G-G'|,\,2\pi/a}$$

Diagonalize at each k, plot eigenvalues vs k: that IS a band structure, produced by the same recipe as VASP's, minus self-consistency and dimensions.

Why the gap equals V0

At the zone edge k = pi/a, two free-electron states — eikx and ei(k - 2pi/a)x — have exactly equal kinetic energy. The potential mixes this degenerate pair; 2x2 degenerate perturbation theory splits them by twice the coupling, i.e. gap = 2 |VG| = 2 (V0/2) = V0. Your run confirms it numerically: gap 3.990 for V0 = 4 (the tiny deficit is higher-order repulsion from farther bands; at V0 = 12 the deviation grows — perturbation theory is a small-V story).

Where the CFL limit r <= 1/2 comes from (the missing derivation)

The explicit update is ui ← ui + r (ui+1 - 2ui + ui-1) with r = alpha dt / dx2. Von Neumann stability analysis asks: what does one step do to a pure error wave uj = ei k x_j? Substituting gives an amplification factor

$$g(k) = 1 - 4r\,\sin^2\!\Big(\frac{k\,dx}{2}\Big)$$

Every mode must satisfy |g| <= 1 or it grows each step. The worst case is the zigzag mode (sin2 = 1, the checkerboard pattern you see in blow-ups), where g = 1 - 4r. Requiring 1 - 4r >= -1 gives r <= 1/2. That is the CFL condition — not a rule of thumb but a theorem about this scheme. Note the cruel scaling: halve dx for accuracy and you must QUARTER dt for stability — 8x the work in 1D. This cost spiral is a big part of why neural surrogates (the AI part of the track) are interesting at all.
